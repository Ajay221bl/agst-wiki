If treatments are randomly assigned, why do we still blind participants and the people measuring the outcomes?

Because random assignment protects the start of the study, not the middle. It makes the groups alike at the moment they're formed. It does nothing about what happens once people know which group they're in. The treated feel better or try to please, the controls lose heart, and raters see what they expect to see. Blinding stops that knowledge from turning into a difference between the groups.

![[Blinding levels.png|700]]
Open label, single-blind and double-blind differ in who knows the group. Each person who knows is another route for expectations to leak into the outcome.

Think of a study as a race to keep exactly one difference between the groups: the treatment.

[[Random Assignment]] wins the first lap. At the moment of assignment, it balances everything about the units, measured or not. The groups start out alike.

But people aren't inert. Once you know your group, it changes how you behave. There's the placebo effect, where believing you're treated makes you feel better. Some subjects try to help the researcher. Some deliberately do the opposite. Some get nervous about being judged. Controls may get discouraged, or try harder to compete. None of this is the treatment, but all of it can show up in the outcome.

The people measuring outcomes are human too. A rater who knows the group can score differently, without meaning to.

Here's the key. All those effects differ by group. So they become a second difference between the groups, alongside the treatment: a confounder created after randomization, which randomization can't touch ([[Nuisance Variables and Confounding]], [[Internal Validity]]).

Blinding, and a placebo, close that gap. If nobody knows who got what, knowledge is the same in every group, and the only systematic difference left is the treatment ([[Blinding and Placebo]]).

Sometimes it's impossible. You can't hide whether someone had surgery or physical therapy. Then you say so, plainly, as a limitation.

The lesson is broader than trials. Expectations are a force. If you don't design them out, they'll show up in your data looking exactly like a result.

Try it yourself. A trial randomly assigns patients to a new pain pill or a sugar pill, but tells each patient which one they got. Is the comparison still fair at the end? Not really. The groups started alike, but people who know they got the real pill may report less pain because they expect to. Randomization can't undo that; only blinding can.
