**In one line:** validity is how far a study's conclusion can be trusted; it is usually split into four kinds: statistical conclusion, internal, construct and external validity.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Experiment]] · [[Population and Sample]] · [[Association vs Causation]]

## Start from zero
1. **A study ends with a claim**: "the treatment raised yield in these fields, and would elsewhere."
2. **That claim has several links, and each can break.** Validity asks, link by link, whether it holds.
3. **Is there really a relationship in the data?** Statistical conclusion validity: right test, [[Test Assumptions|assumptions]] met, enough [[Statistical Power|power]] ([[Statistical Conclusion Validity]]).
4. **Is it causal?** Internal validity: could something other than the treatment explain it ([[Internal Validity]], [[Association vs Causation]])?
5. **Did we measure what we meant?** Construct validity: does the measurement capture the idea ([[Construct Validity]])?
6. **Does it carry over** to other people, places and times? External validity ([[External Validity]]).
7. **Reliability is related but different**: getting the same answer when you repeat the measurement or the study. A measure can be reliable but not valid (consistently wrong).

## Why it exists
"Is this study good?" is too vague to answer. The four kinds name exactly what to check, and show the trade-offs, such as tight control (internal) vs realism (external) ([[Why RCTs trade external validity for internal validity]]).

## A tiny example
A trial run on volunteers at one farm: strong internal validity (randomized), weaker external validity (one farm, volunteers), and its construct validity depends on whether "yield" was measured the same way on every plot.

## Common confusions
- **"Valid means [[Statistical vs Practical Significance|statistically significant]]."** Significance speaks only to statistical conclusion validity.
- **"Reliable means valid."** A scale that is always 2 kg off is reliable and not valid.
