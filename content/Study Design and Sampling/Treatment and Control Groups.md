In an experiment, the **treatment group** gets the thing being tested. The **control group** gets nothing, a placebo or the current standard treatment. The difference between the two groups shows the treatment's effect.

A result means nothing without a comparison. "The plants grew" is not news; "the treated plants grew more than identical untreated ones" is. An experiment changes something on purpose and watches the outcome ([[Experiment]], [[Independent and Dependent Variables]]). But outcomes change anyway: plants grow, patients recover, and people feel better because they expect to. So you need a comparison group treated the same way in every respect except the treatment. Then the difference between the groups is the treatment's effect, not time, the season or expectation. Without a control group, "they got better" can't be separated from "they would have got better anyway".

The groups must be alike at the start. Random assignment makes them alike on average, in things measured and unmeasured ([[Random Assignment]]). Expectations need controlling too: a placebo and blinding give the control group the same beliefs as the treatment group ([[Blinding and Placebo]]). In ANOVA terms, the groups are the levels of one factor, such as treatment A, treatment B and control ([[Factors and Levels]]).

![[Treatment and control groups.png|700]]
Random assignment splits the units; the only planned difference between the groups is the treatment, so the gap in outcomes is its effect.

For example, in a study on sheep with heart damage, 9 got stem cells (treatment) and 9 got a control. Heart pumping capacity fell in the control sheep (mean change −4.3) and rose in the treated sheep (+3.5). The comparison, not the treated group alone, is the evidence ([[Two-Sample Independent t-Test]]).

The control group is your answer to the question "compared to what?" A control group doesn't have to get nothing at all. It can get a placebo or the current standard treatment; what matters is that it differs only in the treatment. And comparing the same units before and after isn't a control: without a separate control group, changes over time aren't ruled out ([[Internal Validity]]).

Try it yourself. A new fertilizer is used on a field and the yield is higher than last year. Without a control group, what else could explain it? Better weather, a different seed batch, or just luck. A control field, treated the same but without the fertilizer, in the same year, would separate those from the fertilizer's effect.
