> [!tip] New to this?
> The single most important idea here is [[Why random assignment and random sampling protect different things]]. Most validity questions come back to it.

What a study can conclude is decided before the data exist.

*Reading:* [[References|Kirk 2013, Ch. 1]], [[References|Vu and Harrington 2020, Ch. 1]] §1.3.

## 1. Asking the question
- [[Population and Sample]]: who I want to know about vs who I measure
- [[Parameter vs Statistic]]: "SS and PP"
- [[PICO Question]]: turning a scientific question into a statistical one
- [[Independent and Dependent Variables]]: what I change, what I measure
- [[Nuisance Variables and Confounding]]: everything else that moves the outcome
- [[Association vs Causation]]: going together vs one changing the other

## 2. Kinds of study
- [[Experiment]]: manipulation plus random assignment
- [[Treatment and Control Groups]]: the comparison that shows what would have happened anyway
- [[Balanced Design]]: equal group sizes, more power and safer tests
- [[Quasi-Experiment]]: one of those is missing
- [[Randomized Controlled Trial]]: the gold standard
- [[Observational Study Designs]]: cohort, case-control, cross-sectional
- [[Blinding and Placebo]]: keeping belief out of the result

## 3. Two kinds of randomness
- [[Random Assignment]]: balances groups, earns causation
- [[Random Sampling]]: represents the population, earns generalization
- [[Sampling Methods]]: SRS, stratified, cluster, systematic and the non-random ones
- [[Sampling Bias]]: a tilt that a bigger n can't fix

## 4. Four kinds of validity
- [[Validity]]: the overview, and validity vs reliability
- [[Internal Validity]]: did the IV cause it?
- [[External Validity]]: how far does it generalize?
- [[Statistical Conclusion Validity]]: are the statistics sound?
- [[Construct Validity]]: am I measuring what I think?
