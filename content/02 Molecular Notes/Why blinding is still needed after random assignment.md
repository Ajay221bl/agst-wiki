If treatments are randomly assigned, why do we still blind participants and the people measuring the outcomes?

Random assignment makes the groups alike at the start. It does nothing about what happens once people know which group they are in. Treated subjects feel better or try to please, controls lose heart, and raters see what they expect. Blinding stops that knowledge from becoming a difference between the groups.

![[Blinding levels.png|700]]
Open label, single-blind, double-blind: who knows the group. Each person who knows is another route for expectations to leak into the outcome.

1. [[Random Assignment]] balances everything about the units, measured or not, at the moment of assignment.
2. After assignment, knowing your group changes behaviour. There is the placebo effect; subjects who try to help the researcher; subjects who deliberately do the opposite; nervousness about being judged; and controls who become discouraged or try to compete.
3. Raters who know the group can score differently, even without meaning to.
4. Those effects differ by group, so they become a second difference besides the treatment: a confounder created after randomization ([[Nuisance Variables and Confounding]], [[Internal Validity]]).
5. Blinding, and a placebo, keep the knowledge equal across groups, so the only systematic difference left is the treatment ([[Blinding and Placebo]]).
6. When blinding is impossible, as with surgery against physical therapy, say so as a limitation.

Otherwise a perfectly randomized trial can still show a "treatment effect" that is really an effect of expectations.
